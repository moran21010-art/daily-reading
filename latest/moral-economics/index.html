The economics of morally ambiguous markets

Controversial transactions test the limits of cost-benefit thinking

David Thesmar

14 MAY 2026 · PAGE 694

Moral Economics

Alvin E. Roth · Basic Venture, 2026. 368 pp.

MORAL ECONOMICS | Moral Economics is a reflection on the moral limits of markets. Its author, Alvin Roth, was co‑recipient of the 2012 Nobel Prize in Economics for his work on “matching markets,” defined by human interactions in which monetary transactions are considered immoral. For instance, he designed algorithms that match doctors to residencies and kidneys to patients, so that a given criterion—the satisfaction of young doctors or hospital administrators, or the number of lives saved— is optimized without resorting to market prices. To put it simply, Roth’s life’s work has been to make these human interactions efficient, while ensuring their moral acceptability.

In this spirit, Moral Economics explores “repugnant” markets, that is, markets in which transactions are contested, prohibited, or morally condemned. Such markets include those surrounding prostitution, assisted dying, surrogacy, recreational drugs, blood transfusion, and so on. Roth discusses each issue in detail, weighing the pros and cons of prohibiting the transactions in question. He often concludes that repugnant markets should be allowed but regulated. The book covers fundamental yet controversial topics and addresses them with depth, intellectual acumen, and an engaging style. It is an important read.

Roth’s book offers an interesting contrast to political philosopher Michael Sandel’s 2012 book What Money Can’t Buy. In that book, Sandel warns that market logic invades human interactions that were previously considered out of reach of market forces. Examples include paying to jump the line for medical services, betting on a stranger’s death through life insurance, and hiring a ghostwriter for a best man speech. Sandel argues that such transactions violate social norms that exist for a reason. In his view, the perception that everything is for sale crowds out our moral sense, diminishes our ability to commit to higher values, and, in the end, degrades societies.

Roth takes the opposite view. Instead of rejecting market transactions on principle, he asks who would benefit from these transactions and who would be harmed. If policy‑makers can figure out a way to maximize benefits and minimize harm, then we should liberalize and regulate, he argues.

This approach is most convincing when the book discusses the kidney exchange program, a smart market design that allows incompatible donor‑recipient pairs to be rearranged so that everyone gives and receives. In this setting, there is no monetary transaction, and no moral sensibility is offended. But in other settings, the economist’s welfarist framework strains. As much as Sandel was writing as an instinctive deontologist, rejecting market interactions as a matter of principle, Roth approaches problems as a pure consequentialist. Yet moral dilemmas cannot always be reduced to the calculus of individual costs and benefits.

Two examples of repugnant markets discussed in the book illustrate the limits of Roth’s consequentialist approach. First, economists may find empirical evidence that legalizing prostitution improves the well‑being of sex workers and their clients. But people who oppose legalization may be concerned that the commodification of sex erodes shared representations (for instance, about the role of women) that bind society together and are worth preserving for their own sake. Second, medical aid in dying may indeed attenuate human suffering. But here again, opponents may worry about the fact that assisted dying becomes a product, an industry, and a career. Such “commodification of death” could, for instance, make it affordable and socially acceptable to gently nudge an old parent to stop being a burden on the rest of the family.

These examples are precisely the kind of moral drift that Sandel’s book is about. But by focusing on empirical evidence, Roth encourages readers to think in terms of cost‑benefit analysis and does not always do full justice to these intuitions. Of course, economic studies are important: They help clarify who gains and who loses from prohibition. But their results are not enough to override people’s moral intuitions.

In the end, Roth’s book may leave readers wanting a finer distinction between cases where the economist’s consequentialist reasoning applies and cases where it does not. Clearly, the kidney exchange program is hard to criticize: The market design makes repugnance disappear, because it substitutes a transaction for a gift exchange. But, sometimes, repugnance is not a bug but a feature—a norm that the community is right to maintain. The question then is: When is cost‑benefit the right framework, and when should we trust the moral instincts of communities?
